    Case Studies — ERPNext in Production¶

    Lessons teach you the pieces. These case studies show what happens when the pieces meet a real business — the decisions, the mistakes, and the results. They are anonymised patterns from production ERPNext work: company types and sizes are real, names are changed, and every number is approximate — "around" and "roughly" mean exactly that, not a measured benchmark.

    Each case follows the same shape: background, the problem, the solution, what went wrong, the results, and what you should take from it. The last two sections of every case are the point: read the solution for ideas, read the mistakes so you do not repeat them.

    Case 1 — A spice manufacturer in Lahore¶

    Background¶

    A small spice manufacturer — roughly 20–30 people, one factory, one warehouse — grinding and packing red chilli powder, turmeric and spice mixes, and selling them to distributors across Punjab. The business had grown past the point where a spreadsheet and a paper register could keep up: around 40–60 sales orders a month, 3–4 raw materials bought in bulk, and every dispatch going out in 1 kg and 5 kg packs.

    This is the same kind of business as the Practice Project factory — same flow, same documents, at real scale instead of demo scale.

    The problem¶

    Three things hurt every week:

    1. Nobody knew what was actually in stock. The storekeeper kept a paper register; the sales team kept a spreadsheet. The two disagreed most days, and the disagreement was only discovered when a customer order could not be fulfilled. A distributor would order 200 packs, the spreadsheet would say they were in stock, and the warehouse would find 140.
    2. Quality was checked after dispatch, not before. A bad batch was found by the customer, not by the factory. One returned consignment meant a lost distributor — and distributors talk to each other.
    3. Nobody knew the real cost of a pack. Raw chilli prices move with the season. The selling price was set by feel, and nobody could say whether a 1 kg pack made a profit in a given month.

    The solution¶

    The core was standard ERPNext, with a small custom app for the one thing ERPNext does not do out of the box:

    • Manufacturing with BOMs and batch-wise stock. Each finished product got a BOM (raw chilli → ground powder → packed product), and every batch of raw material and finished goods was tracked batch-wise, so a quality problem could be traced to one batch instead of the whole month's production. See Lesson 05 — Manufacturing and Lesson 02 — Stock & Inventory.
    • A Quality Inspection gate before dispatch. No Delivery Note could be submitted until the batch passed inspection — a validation in a Server Script that checks the linked Quality Inspection and blocks the submit if it is missing or failed. The gate is one validate hook; the discipline it enforces is the whole point. See Lesson 06 — Quality and Lesson 05 — Server Scripts.
    • Gate Pass for every truck in and out. Raw material arrivals and finished-goods dispatches both passed through the custom Gate Pass DocType from Lesson 03, so the security guard's register and the stock ledger finally agreed with each other.
    • Costing from the BOM, not from feel. The BOM's valuation rate gave the real cost of a pack, and a simple script report compared it against the selling price every month — the first time the owner could see, per product, which packs made money and which did not. See Lesson 08 — Reports & Print Formats.
    • Roles kept the factory floor honest. Storekeepers could create Stock Entries but not submit them; only the production manager could submit. See Lesson 09 — Permissions & Roles.

    What went wrong¶

    1. The quality gate was added too late. The first version let Delivery Notes submit without an inspection, because "we will add the gate after go-live". A bad batch went out in that gap — the exact failure the gate existed to prevent. The fix was to make the gate mandatory from day one and never ship a "we will add it later" control. A control that is not enforced on day one is not a control.
    2. Batch numbers were typed by hand. Two storekeepers typed batch codes differently (BATCH-12 vs batch 12), so the same batch appeared as two batches in reports. The fix was a naming series for batches — the system generates the code, humans never type it. See the field-layout rules in the Common Mistakes guide.

    Results¶

    Roughly, in the first three months: stock disagreements between the register and the system dropped from a weekly argument to a rare exception; no batch went out without a passed inspection after the gate was enforced; and the monthly costing report showed that one spice mix was selling below cost — its price was corrected the same week. None of this was magic. The stock was right because every movement went through one system; the quality gate worked because it blocked the submit instead of asking nicely.

    How we can use it¶

    If you are building for any manufacturer, start with the same three things: batch-wise stock, a submit-blocking quality gate, and a costing report. Build the gate first, before go-live, not after. And never let humans type an identifier the system can generate — naming series exist for exactly this reason.

    Case 2 — A trading and distribution business¶

    Background¶

    A distributor — around 10–15 people, one warehouse, two delivery vans — buying packaged goods from manufacturers and supplying them to shops across a city. Roughly 150–200 deliveries a month, most of them small, most of them on credit. The business lived or died on two things: knowing what each shop owed, and knowing where each van was.

    The problem¶

    1. Credit was given by memory. The owner knew which shops paid on time and which did not — until he went on leave, and nobody else knew. Goods went out to shops that were already months behind on payment.
    2. Deliveries were tracked by phone call. The office called the driver, the driver called the shop, the shop said "not yet". A delivery that was actually made could sit as "pending" for days, and an invoice could not be raised against a pending delivery.
    3. Warehouse in/out had no paper trail. Goods left the warehouse without a document, so when stock went missing there was no way to say when, or on whose watch.

    The solution¶

    Almost entirely standard ERPNext — this case is mostly about using the standard flow properly instead of working around it:

    • The standard order-to-cash flow, enforced end to end. Sales Order → Delivery Note → Sales Invoice → Payment Entry, with no step skipped. An invoice could only be created from a submitted Delivery Note, so "delivered but not invoiced" stopped being a category. See Lesson 03 — Selling and Lesson 01 — Accounting.
    • Credit limits on every Customer. Each shop got a credit limit based on its payment history, and the system blocked new Sales Orders over the limit — the rule no longer lived in one person's head. Overdue invoices got a daily email to the owner through a scheduled report. See Lesson 11 — Scheduler & Background Jobs.
    • Delivery tracking from the Delivery Note itself. The driver's name and van went on the Delivery Note, and the submitted Delivery Note was the proof of delivery — the shop signed the print format, and the signed copy came back to the office. See Lesson 08 — Reports & Print Formats.
    • Gate Pass for warehouse movements. Every load leaving the warehouse got a Gate Pass linked to its Delivery Notes, so the guard's register matched the stock ledger at the end of the day. This is the same Gate Pass from Lesson 03, reused in a completely different business.
    • A simple dashboard. Accounts Receivable ageing, today's deliveries, and overdue customers on one Insights dashboard the owner checked every morning. See Lesson 18 — Insights.

    What went wrong¶

    1. The credit limit was set too high at first. The limits were copied from the old "gut feeling" numbers, which were generous. The system enforced them perfectly — and perfectly enforced a bad policy. The fix was to set limits from actual payment history (average monthly payment, not the biggest month), and to review them every quarter. Automation enforces the rule you write, not the rule you meant.
    2. Drivers resisted the signed Delivery Note. The paper copy felt like extra work, and for two weeks vans left without it. The fix was not technical: the owner made the signed copy the condition for the driver's daily allowance being counted. The process stuck after that. A system change that nobody follows is not a system change — plan the human part, not just the DocType.

    Results¶

    Roughly: overdue receivables fell noticeably within a couple of months, because new orders to late payers simply stopped being created; the "delivered but not invoiced" list, which used to run to dozens of entries, went to near zero — an invoice now follows the delivery the same day; and end-of-day stock reconciliation, which used to take the storekeeper an hour of phone calls, became a five-minute comparison of two lists that already agreed.

    How we can use it¶

    For any trading business, the standard flow is the product — do not customise before you have used it as designed for a month. Credit limits belong in the system, not in one person's memory, and they must come from payment history, not from optimism. And when a new step meets resistance, fix the incentive, not the form.

    Case 3 — A services company¶

    Background¶

    A small services company — around 8–12 people — doing client projects: implementation work, support contracts, and the occasional fixed-price job. The work was tracked in a spreadsheet, the hours were tracked in a chat group, and the invoices were written from memory at the end of the month. Roughly 6–8 active projects at any time, each with a different client, a different rate, and a different idea of what "done" meant.

    The problem¶

    1. Nobody knew if a project made money. Hours were recorded late or not at all, so the cost of a project was a guess — and the invoice was a bigger guess.
    2. Approvals happened in chat. A discount, a free extra, a change of scope — all agreed in a message, all forgotten by invoice time. The client remembered the promise; the company could not find it.
    3. Clients could not see anything. Every status question came as a phone call or a message: "where is my project?", "when is the next invoice?" The team answered the same questions every week.

    The solution¶

    ERPNext Projects, with timesheets doing the heavy lifting:

    • One Project per engagement, with Tasks underneath. Every piece of work was a Task with an owner and a due date, and every hour was a Timesheet entry linked to the Task. The project's cost became the sum of its Timesheet entries — no more guessing. See Lesson 07 — Projects.
    • Sales Invoices from Timesheets. Billable hours were pulled straight from approved Timesheets into the invoice, so the invoice matched the work actually done. Fixed-price projects were invoiced against milestones instead. See Lesson 01 — Accounting.
    • An approval Workflow for anything that costs money. Discounts, scope changes and free work went through a Workflow on the Sales Order — requested by the project lead, approved by the owner — so every promise existed as a document, not a chat message. See Lesson 10 — Workflows & Assignments.
    • A client portal for status and tickets. Clients logged into the portal to see their project's progress and to raise support tickets, instead of calling. Tickets went through the Helpdesk, so nothing was lost in a chat thread. See Lesson 12 — Support & Portal and Lesson 19 — Website & Portal.
    • A weekly project report. Hours logged, hours billed, and project margin per client, emailed to the owner every Monday morning by a scheduled job. See Lesson 11 — Scheduler & Background Jobs.

    What went wrong¶

    1. Timesheets were filled on Friday, from memory. The team logged a week's hours in one sitting, which meant the hours were approximate — and "approximate" always rounded up. The fix was a daily reminder at 5 pm and a rule: a Timesheet more than two days old needs the project lead's approval before it can be billed. Hours got accurate when they got daily.
    2. The portal launched before anyone told the clients. The portal was live for a month and almost nobody used it, because no client had been told it existed or given a login. The fix was a short email to every active client with their login and one line — "your project's status is here now" — and the phone calls dropped within a week. A feature nobody is told about is a feature that does not exist.

    Results¶

    Roughly: the monthly invoice stopped being a negotiation, because every line on it pointed at an approved Timesheet or a milestone; one project was found to be running at a loss halfway through, and its scope was renegotiated instead of discovered at the end; and the weekly "where is my project?" calls fell away once clients could see the status themselves. The margin report did the most work of all — it turned "are we making money?" from a feeling into a number the owner checked every Monday.

    How we can use it¶

    For any services business, the Timesheet is the source of truth — everything else (invoice, margin, project status) is derived from it, so protect its accuracy first. Put every money decision through a Workflow, because a promise made in chat is a dispute waiting to happen. And when you build a portal, launching it is the easy half; telling the clients is the other half.

    What the three cases have in common¶

    1. The standard flow did most of the work. None of these businesses needed a big custom app. The manufacturing case needed one gate and one custom DocType; the trading case needed almost nothing custom; the services case needed a Workflow and a portal. Use the standard flow first, customise second.
    2. Every control that mattered blocked something. The quality gate blocked the submit, the credit limit blocked the order, the approval Workflow blocked the discount. A control that only warns is a control that gets ignored.
    3. The human part decided whether it worked. Drivers needed an incentive to carry the signed copy; timesheets needed a daily reminder; clients needed an email to use the portal. The DocType is half the implementation — the other half is the people who use it.
    4. The mistakes were all avoidable — and all familiar. A gate added too late, limits copied from gut feeling, hours logged from memory, a portal nobody was told about. If you read these cases and think "we would never do that", read them again after your first go-live.

    Contribute your own case study¶

    These three cases are a start, not a catalogue. If you have shipped ERPNext in a real business — a factory, a shop, a school, a clinic, a farm — write it up in this same shape and send it in. Anonymise the names, keep the numbers approximate and honest, and say what went wrong: the mistakes are the most useful part. See CONTRIBUTING.md for how to submit.
